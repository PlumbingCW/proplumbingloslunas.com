A railroad town with plumbing to match

Belen was incorporated in 1918 and calls itself the Hub City because of the railroad. About 7,360 people live here, and BNSF moves roughly 110 trains a day through town. The Harvey House, the Rail Runner station and the old downtown near Main Street show how much of Belen predates modern plumbing code.

Older commercial and residential buildings downtown often have cast iron, clay or galvanized lines. These fail slowly and then all at once. If you own a downtown building, a camera inspection is the cheapest way to see what is under the floor.

Water you can use and what the city does to it

The city runs its own wells, with several near the interstate. Belen treats its water for arsenic at a treatment plant at the Cavco well site, which is why you see the city talk about arsenic in its annual report. At the tap, the more common household effects are scale and sediment.

That is hard on water heaters. If yours is loud, slow to heat or leaking at the base, see water heater replacement, or repair when the tank is still sound.

Rail traffic, vibration and old pipe

Trains pass through Belen all day, and vibration can work on old joints in buildings close to the tracks. Cast iron and clay drains already near the end of their life can fail sooner. If you own a building near the corridor, a camera look at the drain is a smart check.
