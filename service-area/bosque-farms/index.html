Plumbing in a village built on a farm settlement

Bosque Farms was incorporated in 1974, but its roots go back to 1935, when the federal government resettled Dust Bowl farm families on small irrigated tracts here. Roughly 4,020 people live in the village today. Many lots are large, and a lot of houses sit well back from the street.

That layout matters for plumbing. Long runs from the street to the house mean longer water service lines, and a leak in one can run unnoticed under a yard for weeks. If your bill jumps with no change in use, ask us about leak detection before the lawn turns soggy.

How the village water and sewer system affects your repair

The village draws its water from two wells and treats it with chlorine. A homeowner sees the same things anywhere on municipal water: mineral scale at the tap, and a water heater that works harder as sediment builds up. Flushing the tank once a year helps, and our water heater repair crews can show you how.

The sewer side is shared with a neighbor. Bosque Farms treats the wastewater that Peralta sends its way, so the system serves more than the village alone. When a main line backs up on your street, it helps to call us first so a plumber can tell whether the blockage is in your lateral or in the public line.

Before we arrive: a burst pipe or active leak in Bosque Farms

Take these steps while the plumber is on the way.

Shut off the main valve

On many Bosque Farms homes the main sits near the front of the house or at the meter box. Turn it clockwise.

Kill power to wet areas

Flip breakers for rooms with standing water. Stay out if water is touching outlets.

Clear the immediate area

Lift rugs, boxes and electronics away from the water. Open a low faucet to relieve pressure.

Worth knowing in Bosque Farms
